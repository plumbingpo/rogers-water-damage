Attic Water Damage Help in Rogers

There are two ways an attic ends up wet. Rain gets in from above through worn shingles, failed flashing, or the seals around vents and chimneys. Moisture also rises from the house below and condenses on cold roof sheathing when the attic is not ventilated well. In both cases, almost nobody climbs up to check. For Rogers homeowners, our crew examines the attic, identifies where the water comes from, and dries the space.

Most attic jobs center on wet insulation. Fiberglass and cellulose both lose R-value when they get wet, so the home costs more to heat and cool. The bigger problem is that for weeks, saturated insulation holds moisture on the drywall of the ceiling and on the framing. Our process is to pull the saturated insulation, dry the wood using dehumidifiers and air movers, and treat mold found on rafters or sheathing.

Signs to Look For

  • Water dripping from, or staining around, a ceiling light or bathroom fan
  • Musty odor in upstairs closets or in the attic itself
  • After rain, a stain or brown ring on a top floor ceiling that looks worse
  • Dark stains or streaks on the rafters and the underside of the roof deck
  • Water droplets or rust on the tips of nails that poke through the roof sheathing
  • Insulation that looks matted, flattened, or darker than the insulation around it
Behind the Surface

The Damage You Do Not See

Water Near Electrical

Attics hold junction boxes, recessed light housings and cable runs. Do not enter the attic before someone checks the power, because wet electrical parts can shock you or start a fire.

Mold Under the Roof Deck

Poorly ventilated attics often grow mold across the underside of the sheathing. It can cover a wide area before anyone goes up to look.

Bath Fans Venting Inside

A bathroom fan that vents into the attic instead of outdoors adds warm, moist air every day. On cold sheathing that air condenses, and the result is easy to mistake for a leaking roof.

Lost Insulation Value

Once damp and packed down, insulation does little of its job and might not recover even when dry. Rooms under the wet section run hotter or colder, and energy bills rise.

How We Fix It

How We Handle Attic Water Damage

1

Inspect and Trace

Our inspection covers the flashing points, roof deck, exhaust ducts and vents, and tells us if the water is condensation, a leak, or both. How far it spread is shown by moisture meters and thermal imaging.

2

Take Out Wet Insulation

Saturated or moldy insulation is bagged and removed so the wood and drywall under it can dry. Rooms under the attic are protected, and the route in and out stays contained.

3

Dry the Framing

Rafters, sheathing and joists are dried by air movers and dehumidifiers until their moisture levels are normal. Visible mold is cleaned from the wood and an antimicrobial is applied.

4

Correct the Source

You are shown the ventilation or roof problem to be corrected, and our findings can go to your roofer. New insulation is installed to bring back the R-value after the attic is staying dry.

Why Speed Matters

How the Damage Progresses

The first storm

Insulation Soaks It Up

Rain that gets past the roof lands in the insulation, which absorbs it. Nothing shows indoors, so the leak goes unnoticed and the roof is not repaired.

Hours 24 to 48

Mold May Begin

In a still, warm attic, paper facing and wood that stay damp give mold ideal conditions. The sheathing, along with the top face of the drywall ceiling, is where growth can begin.

Weeks later

Ceiling Stains Appear

Once storm after storm has saturated the insulation, the ceiling starts getting wet. In the room underneath, brown rings show up and the drywall may begin sagging.

Months later

Wood Starts Rotting

Sheathing delaminates and loses its hold on the roofing nails. Large areas of roof decking are covered in mold, while rafters that never dry begin to decay.
